Fifteen minutes, run to the minute

For eight years I have run fast, small sites: quick-service restaurants in the UK and a factory floor in India. A burger ticket and a fifteen-minute grocery order run on the same physics: stock on the shelf before the order arrives, a clean pick, a checked bag, a rider who never waits. This page follows one order through those minutes and shows where my work fits.

00:00Order placed

Availability is decided before the order exists

Fifteen minutes leaves no time to find stock. It is set hours earlier, in the forecast and the replenishment plan.

What I would watch: items offline, short picks, forecast against actual by hour.

From my workAt Shake Shack Birmingham I set the daily and weekly sales forecasts myself and planned prep and deployment to them. Items offline on the delivery apps was one of the numbers I reviewed every week.

02:00Picking

Speed at the pick face comes from layout and habit

Most lost seconds sit in walking, searching and waiting. They show up in throughput data long before anyone complains.

What I would watch: units picked per hour, mispicks, time from order drop to pick start.

From my workI read daily POS, labour and throughput figures to remove service bottlenecks, and have run Kaizen events at all four of my employers. At Fresh Bake, 5S cut the waiting time on made-to-order items.

05:00Pack and check

A missing item costs more than the item

A refund, a complaint and a customer less sure next time. The cheapest fix is a check at the bench before the bag is sealed.

What I would watch: missing and wrong items per thousand orders, refunds by cause.

From my workI checked bags, seals and items before every rider hand-off at Shake Shack. I also built a multi-site tracker that logged each complaint and refund by shift, platform, value and lost revenue.

07:00Rider hand-off

Riders should never wait, and orders should never wait for riders

The hand-off is where the store's minutes become the rider's minutes. Peak hour is where it breaks.

What I would watch: rider wait time, orders ready with no rider, no-shows by hour.

From my workI ran the rider hand-off daily across three delivery platforms. That meant the waiting area and flow at peak, chasing late or no-show riders with the platform, and sorting out disputes and wrong collections on the spot.

13:00On the road

The store does not drive, but it owns what left the door

Ratings and refunds arrive after the order, and they point back to a stage inside the site.

What I would watch: on-time rate, ratings and refunds, traced back to the shift and stage.

From my workEvery week I reviewed prep time, platform ratings, refunds and missing items, and items offline. Our ratings typically sat at 4.5 stars or above.

15:00Doorstep

The customer only sees the last second

Whether they order again depends on fifteen minutes they never saw.

What I would watch: repeat orders and contact rate, by neighbourhood and site.

From my workAt Shake Shack Birmingham, guest likelihood to return rose from 8.1 to 9.3 after we rebuilt deployment, floor plans and shift handovers.

Four cases

Pressure, systems, method and people

Pressure · Shake Shack Birmingham · 28 May 2025

International Burger Day, with a delivery at the back door

We ran buy-one-get-one-free on every burger, and the queue had formed behind the shutter by 10:30. At 11:00, as we opened, a delivery worth about £4,000 arrived. Fresh and frozen stock could not sit outside. I took the two slower team members off the expo station, where orders are checked and handed out, to put it away. Then I ran expo alone, with 15 to 20 orders on the screen.

It held because most of the team could work most stations, and people came across to help when their own station allowed. By 11:30 the stock was away. I stepped off the stations to manage the floor, restock and run the temperature checks. We took about £4,500 by 15:00 and about £11,000 that day.

What it taught: cross-training is what saves the worst day, and someone has to stand back and see the whole floor.

Systems · Fresh Bake, Leicester · 2024

One fix the whole team could run without me

A couple who could neither hear nor speak came in, both allergic to dairy. Ordering took a long time and risked a mistake. I gave them pen and paper and showed them each ingredient one by one, and served an iced latte with oat milk and an iced mocha with almond milk.

After their second visit I put their order on a note at the counter and briefed the team. From then on anyone could serve them in seconds, safely. On my last shift they asked for a photo with me.

What it taught: a good fix is one the next shift can repeat without you.

Method · Vikram Engineering Works, Gujarat

A fault that came back with the seasons

Surface roughness on our spinning rings drifted with the seasons, and customers noticed. I used a fishbone across man, machine, method and material to separate the causes, then tested a technical fix on the line.

The fix did not remove every variation, and it did not need to. It stabilised output enough that the complaints fell away.

What it taught: problem-solving is a spectrum. Solving most of a problem, and measuring it, still counts.

People · Shake Shack Birmingham

He showed me how to manage him

One team member worked slowly, and I was ready to end his probation. After a shift I talked to him as a colleague, and he told me about a condition I had not known about. He explained how to hand him tasks, and I built his shifts around his strengths.

When the area manager later wanted him dismissed over one wrong order, I asked for a month. Two months after our talk he won Employee of the Month.

What it taught: before deciding someone is slow, ask what would make them fast.

Leadership Principles

Six moments from my work, matched to six principles

Ownership

Supplier prices, after hours

At Fresh Bake I believed the uniform and ingredient quotes could be beaten. I researched local suppliers after my shifts and said nothing until I had a better price; the Operations Director checked it and switched.

Dive Deep

Time it before you hand it over

I do each task myself first and time it. Then I hand it to the team with a time limit and coaching, and the shift runs with fewer people.

Learn and Be Curious

The first day at Greggs

New to the UK, a supervisor walked out and I was alone at the front. The accent and the pace were hard. I nearly called to quit that night, then went back and learned both.

Hire and Develop the Best

The interview that did not go well

A young man who had just arrived in the UK struggled in his Fresh Bake interview. I remembered my own first week and hired him. After I left, he became the store's Assistant Manager.

Insist on the Highest Standards

Everyone tastes everything

Before Shake Shack Birmingham opened, each team member made and rated every item. On shift I asked spot questions and rewarded right answers with recognition cards.

Earn Trust

A card from every team member

When I left, every single team member wrote in my farewell card.
